# How research415 writes

Matt reads every thread, review and post about the client and their competitors, then sends one written report. The report has to read like a sharp person who did that reading and formed a view, talking to a founder they respect. Not like a research document, not like a newsletter, and never like a chatbot.

The reader is a founder or a marketing lead on a phone between meetings. Every line either tells them something they can act on, or explains why a thing matters to their business, or it goes.

## 1. The outcome rule

Every finding, cell and bullet ends on what the reader gets or what to do. A sentence that ends on an observation is unfinished.

- Unfinished: "...the searches that describe the category belong to nobody yet."
- Finished: "...the searches that describe the category belong to nobody yet. Take them with one page per term and cheap clicks, before Wispr's paid search hire starts."

Judge every fact from the client's side. It is good, bad or neutral for them, and the text says which. No neutral industry commentary.

Say what a thing costs and what it returns, in the client's terms: an afternoon of work, one email to an existing list, a trial from someone already sold. A recommended action in a weekly ends with a sentence starting "Outcome:".

## 2. Plain words

Write for a smart person who has never worked in marketing or software. Say what a thing is, not what it is called.

- "Free Google results", not "organic SERP".
- "The security audit big companies ask vendors for (SOC 2)", not "SOC 2 Type II attestation".
- "Pre-paid units the AI uses up as it works", not "consumption-based credit model".
- "People who left and said where they went", not "churn signals".
- "How many days an ad ran", not "creative longevity".

Define a term in half a sentence the first time it appears, then use it freely. If a phrase sounds clever, replace it with what it means. No metaphors, no coined phrases: not "creator machine", "war chest", "narrative", "leak", "cheerleaders", "lit up X", "wide open", "pick the fight accordingly", "trojan horse".

## 3. Sentences

- First sentence of every paragraph is the conclusion. The numbers follow.
- Short. One idea per sentence. Split anything that wants a semicolon.
- Active voice. "Viktor doubled its cheapest plan." Not "It appears Viktor may have increased pricing."
- No em dashes, no en dashes, no semicolons, no curly quotes, no emoji in our own words. Colons only after a bold lead-in and in table cells used as labels ("Viktor: none. Adapt: limits per person").
- No filler: "it is worth noting", "interestingly", "in order to", "leverage", "utilize", "robust", "ecosystem", "landscape", "delve", "pivotal", "seamless", "crucial", "Moreover", "Additionally", "Furthermore".
- No adjectives that do not carry a number. "7 of 9 August G2 reviews complain about credit costs" beats "customers are unhappy with pricing".
- "About", never "approximately". Dates as "Aug 20". Numbers as "$176K", "646K", "13,810". Ranges with "to": "$6 to 20". Percentages as "29%".
- The competitor and the client by brand name, one name each, all the way through. People by full name and role the first time, then surname or role.
- Second person is fine in advice cells and recommendations ("Reply today", "Y'all should bid on it"). Evidence paragraphs stay in the third person. Never first person.

## 4. Quotes stay as written

The Post cell is a quote, not a summary. Keep typos, lowercase, profanity and the original language, with a translation in square brackets after it. "Why is Wispr flow so shit these days?" stays exactly as written. Trim to the part that matters, and mark trims with an ellipsis. Never paraphrase a customer into marketing language, and never adopt the competitor's marketing words ("seamless", "powerful") as our own. Attribute them: "Wispr's pricing page says".

## 5. Advice is decided

Evidence can be uncertain. Advice cannot. "Consider reaching out", "it might be worth", "you may want to" all mean the writer has not decided. Decide.

- Before: "It could be worth considering a reply here."
- After: "Reply today. This is hours, not days."

Every advice cell tells the reader what to do or tells them to skip it. "Not worth chasing." "Competitor, don't pitch." "Reply in French." "A 20-second video wins this thread." Lead with the verb, then the one fact that makes it land. Twenty words, twenty-five at the outside, in a daily. Read the last column of a table straight down: if every cell has the same rhythm and the same verb, rewrite three of them. Let one be four words.

Two things that are not hedging: a stated condition where a product fact is unknown ("If Aqua ships a Shortcuts action, say so"), and a softened ask on a decision that is the client's alone, meaning pricing, roadmap or headcount ("Consider a two-week trial" is honest where "Raise the free tier to 5,000 words" is us inventing a number). The softening lives in the action line only. The evidence under it stays specific.

Never claim a product detail about the client that has not been confirmed. Write "if [client] does X, say so."

## 6. Numbers and estimates

- Every number has a source pill or a clause saying where it came from. A number nothing covers gets cut.
- Traffic, spend and revenue from outside tools are estimates. Say so next to the number, once, in the paragraph that carries it: "Similarweb puts it at 4.4M visits, which is an estimate." Never in a footer wall.
- When two tools disagree by more than 2x, print both. "SpyFu says $176K, Semrush $82K. The real number is probably between the two."
- Revenue a company claims about itself is "what the founders say".
- No fake precision. "Roughly 910" and "946" from two counts are both fine, printed together. "Approximately 927.5" is not.
- Never call a trend off two days of ad-library data. Meta indexes the newest days late.
- Complaint volume shows who is reachable and why they are unhappy. It is not evidence about whether the competitor keeps its customers. Never write a sentence implying rising complaints mean the competitor is losing users.

## 7. What never appears in a client's report

- The name of a tool that fetched something: Apify, an actor, a scraper, Firecrawl, a browser pane, a session, a credit limit, "our pull failed". The pill for an X post is "X". A reader judging a claim gets nothing from the vendor in our pipeline.
- A methodology paragraph, a caveat wall, a footer explaining what could not be reached. If a source is missing, say what is missing in one clause next to the affected number: "No X counts this week, so the objection mix is counted from G2 reviews." Why our tooling failed is our problem.
- Character judgments about the competitor. "Not organic" when the numbers show it. Never "dishonest".
- A section padded to fill the template. If the ads section has nothing worth acting on, it is two sentences. A short section is honest. A padded one costs trust on every other section.
- The same person or post in two tables.
- A row the reader cannot act on: wrong language for the market, wrong time zone, a platform they have no account on, a competitor's own staff. Those go in the run log under "Seen but not sent", not in the report.
- An action that argues from an absence ("Viktor has not posted a role for X, so...") or needs a hire the client does not have, or that the evidence in the report does not directly support.

## 8. The patterns our own drafts produce

These have shipped. Check for them first.

1. **The same callout opener four times in a row.** "**Why it matters for Cloudless:**" is fine once or twice in a section. On every finding it reads like a form. Open the others on the point itself: "**You can get these two back, and they told you exactly what to fix.**"
2. **Advice cells that describe.** "He is a paying customer who has been complaining about accuracy for a month." is a research note. "Nobody has answered him and he hasn't committed yet. Send the accuracy page and a free month." is a to-do.
3. **Rows that all sound the same.** Five rows written in one sitting come out with one rhythm. Vary length and verb.
4. **The caveat wall.** A limitations paragraph at the end of a section. Move each caveat next to its number or cut it.
5. **Negative parallelism as a tic.** "Not X but Y", "this is the warning, not the target". One per report is fine. Two is a pattern.
6. **Superficial -ing clauses.** "...highlighting their growing focus on international markets." Cut the clause, state the fact: "They launched seven ads in Japan on Aug 30. It is the first time they have advertised there."
7. **Inflated significance.** "marks a pivotal moment", "underscores", "reflects a broader shift", "setting the stage". State what happened with the date and the number.
8. **Vague attribution.** "users report", "many people say", "observers note". Every claim about what people think needs a count or a linked post.
9. **Copula avoidance.** "serves as", "stands as", "represents", "boasts". Say "is".
10. **False ranges.** "Everything from pricing pages to hiring signals to ad spend." Name the three things.
11. **Elegant variation.** Alternating "Wispr", "the company", "the incumbent", "the category leader". One name.
12. **Generic positive ending.** "The opportunity here is significant and the timing looks favourable." End on the move: "Go now and you're the first paid voice in Japanese dictation. Wait a month and you're the third."
13. **Fake precision and invented reach.** If a tool did not return a number, leave the column out or write what the number rests on. Never invent upvotes.

## 9. What good looks like

From a shipped brief, four rules working at once:

> **Don't chase him.** Wispr paid for the biggest name on the list and got less reach than a guy with 2,000 followers. He's here as a warning about where the money goes.

Plain words. One concrete comparison with both numbers. Opens on the instruction. Ends on the reason. No first person, and it still sounds like somebody who looked at the data and formed a view.

And a weekly action:

> **Ask your existing users to leave a review on the App Store.** Cloudless has 2 iPhone ratings. Wispr has 14,620 and added about 590 of them last week. Nobody picks the app with 2 ratings when the other one has fourteen thousand. You already have emails for your existing users and 2,314 people who claimed a free seat. Outcome: a few hundred ratings, and the listing stops losing people before they ever open the app.

## 10. The ship checklist

Run the checker, then read the draft as the founder would.

```
python3 tools/report.py check <client> <slug>
```

Fix every error. Read every warning and fix it unless you can say why the exception is right.

Then by eye:

- [ ] The first thing on the page tells the reader what to do today. They could act without scrolling.
- [ ] Every advice cell ends on an instruction or on "skip it". None ends on a description.
- [ ] No hedged advice. Uncertainty lives in the evidence, never in the recommendation.
- [ ] Read the last table column straight down. The rows do not share a rhythm.
- [ ] Every quote is the poster's own words, typos included, original language kept.
- [ ] Every number has a pill or a clause saying where it came from. Caveats sit next to their number.
- [ ] No two callouts in a section open the same way.
- [ ] No section padded to fill the template. No small print, no methodology, no footer on a daily.
- [ ] The competitor has one name throughout. No tool is named anywhere.
- [ ] Every row is one the reader can act on, in the client's market and language.
- [ ] Nothing from yesterday's report reappears unless the thread grew.
- [ ] Nothing reads like a newsletter, an essay, or a chatbot reply.
- [ ] A daily is a five to ten minute read, 1,200 to 2,000 words. A weekly is 8,000 to 10,000 rendered.
